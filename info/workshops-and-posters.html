Workshops

Both workshops are 90 minutes long.

Workshop 1: The Best of Both Worlds—Beyond Unplugged

Workshop leaders: Annika Vielsack (Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, Germany), Beatrice Deutschmann (unaffiliated)

Abstract: The idea of Computer Science Unplugged has emerged in the early 1990s and has gained widespread international recognition since. It provides an effective way to engage learners of all ages in core concepts of computer science. However, purely unplugged approaches also face limitations, particularly when addressing more abstract or complex topics. One promising way to address these limitations is to enrich unplugged activities with carefully selected digital tools, while preserving the core principles of unplugged learning.

In this workshop, we present such a hybrid approach by introducing an activity that combines a serious game in the form of a WebApp with an unplugged activity to teach Satisfiability and the DPLL Algorithm – an advanced topic from the field of theoretical informatics – to secondary school students.

Participants of the proposed workshop will experience the activity firsthand. Furthermore, they are invited to reflect on their own teaching or outreach activities and to identify concrete opportunities for enhancing existing activities through the integration of additional plugged or unplugged elements.

Keywords: CS unplugged, Computer Science Education, Satisfiability, DPLL, Theoretical Informatics

Workshop 2: What is a "Good" Honours Project in Computing?

Workshop leaders: Mark Zarb (Robert Gordon University, UK), Steve Riddle (Newcastle University, UK)

Abstract: Individual project modules remain a cornerstone of Computing education, yet the context in which they are delivered is changing rapidly. Growing cohort sizes, shifting student preparedness, and the widespread availability of generative AI tools all challenge established models of supervision, assessment, and academic integrity.

This workshop asks a deceptively simple question: what makes a "good" Honours Project in Computing? Moving beyond alignment with marking criteria, we invite participants to examine this question holistically, considering authenticity, student development, and sustainability. Drawing on a draft typological framework developed through an ongoing collaborative project among UK Computing educators, participants will situate their own institutional approaches, critically evaluate the framework, and contribute to its refinement. Participants will leave with comparative insight, a refined framework for use as a reflective or benchmarking tool, and an invitation to engage with the broader ongoing research agenda.

Keywords: Education, Project-based learning, Assessment, Authentic assessment, Dissertation
